About Diabetes

This is a student summary of public health sources (WHO, CDC, NIDDK) listed on the Project page.

What is diabetes?

Insulin is a hormone that lets glucose enter body cells for energy. In diabetes the body makes too little insulin or cannot use it well, so blood glucose stays too high. Over time this can damage the heart, kidneys, eyes and nerves.

Type 1

The immune system damages insulin-producing cells, so the body makes little or no insulin. It often appears in children and young adults and needs daily insulin treatment.

Type 2

The body becomes resistant to insulin or does not make enough. It is the most common type and is linked to weight, inactivity, age and family history. It often develops slowly.

Gestational

High blood glucose first found during pregnancy. It usually goes away after birth but raises the mother's future risk of Type 2 diabetes.

Common symptoms

  • Frequent urination, increased thirst
  • Increased hunger and tiredness
  • Blurred vision
  • Slow-healing wounds
  • Unexplained weight loss

Some people, especially with Type 2, have no early symptoms.

Risk factors (Type 2)

  • Overweight or obesity
  • Physical inactivity
  • Family history
  • Increasing age
  • High blood pressure
  • Gestational diabetes in the past

Prevention and lifestyle

  • Stay physically active
  • Eat a balanced diet; limit sugary drinks
  • Maintain a healthy weight
  • Avoid tobacco

Lifestyle changes can prevent or delay Type 2 diabetes.

Why see a doctor early?

Diabetes can be confirmed only by blood tests. Early detection and treatment reduce complications. Do not self-diagnose or change medicines without medical advice.
